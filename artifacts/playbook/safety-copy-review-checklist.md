### 16. `lit-shared-if-you-dont-feel-safe` — SHARED

- [ ] Reviewed
- *Type:* literature entry · **Appears in:** `breaking-the-cycle`, `finding-your-way-back`, `staying-connected`

> If you don't feel safe
>
> We're putting this first because it matters more than anything else here.
>
> People in difficult relationships often say some version of: I don't feel emotionally safe. I don't feel safe enough to be honest. I'm careful about how I say things.
>
> Sometimes that means two people who handle conflict badly and both feel bruised afterwards. Sometimes it means something else.
>
> **None of the following is a communication problem:**
>
> – Being frightened of how they'll react.
>
> – Changing what you say to manage their temper.
>
> – Being made to feel small, stupid, or unstable on purpose.
>
> – Being blamed for things that happened to you.
>
> – Having your access to money, people, or leaving controlled.
>
> – Being pressured into anything physical.
>
> – Anything physical, ever.
>
> Every tool in this Playbook asks you to raise difficult things and stay in the conversation. If any of the above is happening, that is the wrong instruction and following it could make things worse. Please talk to someone who can help you look at it properly — a domestic abuse service, a GP, or a therapist. You don't have to be certain it's serious to ask.
>
> If none of that fits, and it's genuinely two people who find this hard — the rest of this is for you.

## P3 · Bereavement, grief & loss  (17)

The add-on grief material the README flags as sensitive. Review for tone, accuracy, and that it never oversteps into specialist grief / fertility-loss territory it isn't scoped for.

### 25. `lit-bereave-changes`

- [ ] Reviewed
- *Type:* literature entry · in `addon-losing-a-partner`

> Why does it change so much day to day?
>
> “My grief changes from day to day.” It does, and the unpredictability is one of the more exhausting parts — partly because it makes planning anything difficult.
>
> **What people expect, and what happens:** Expected: a gradual decline. Bad, then less bad, then manageable.
>
> Actual: much less orderly. A good fortnight followed by a worse week than any of the first month, often set off by something small and unrelated.
>
> The practical consequence: a bad day isn't evidence of going backwards, and a good fortnight isn't evidence of being through it. Both readings are common and both cause a lot of unnecessary alarm.
>
> “My world changed overnight” is worth naming as its own thing. Sudden loss and expected loss are different experiences, and people who had no warning often find that the shock and the grief are two separate processes running at different speeds.

**Decision:** 

### 26. `lit-bereave-companionship`

- [ ] Reviewed
- *Type:* literature entry · in `addon-losing-a-partner`

> Wanting company
>
> “I want companionship, but I don't know if my heart is ready.” “Dating again feels like I'm betraying them.” “I don't want to replace them.”
>
> That last sentence contains the whole difficulty, and it rests on an assumption worth looking at.
>
> **Replacing and adding are different:** Replacement assumes a single slot — that a new person would occupy the place the last one had, and therefore displace them.
>
> Most people who do this find it doesn't work like that. The new relationship occupies its own place, and the previous one isn't vacated. People routinely hold both.
>
> “I don't know how to love someone new while honouring my past” is the right question, and the answer most people arrive at is unglamorous: by not hiding it. Relationships that begin with the previous one acknowledged tend to hold both more easily than ones where it's managed around.
>
> None of this is an argument for dating. Wanting companionship and not wanting to date are both entirely coherent positions, and so is wanting neither.

**Decision:** 

### 27. `lit-bereave-faq-ready`

- [ ] Reviewed
- *Type:* literature entry · in `addon-losing-a-partner`

> How do I know if I'm ready?
>
> There's no threshold, and the people who tell you there is are guessing. Some people are ready in months and some never want to be, and neither says anything about the marriage.
>
> **Two questions that are more useful than readiness:** Would I be able to talk about them? Not hide the marriage, not manage around it. If the answer is no, that's worth knowing before rather than after.
>
> Am I wanting company, or wanting the ache to stop? Both are legitimate. They lead to different decisions.
>
> “I don't know if I'll ever stop missing them” probably has the answer no, and that isn't the obstacle it looks like. Missing someone permanently is compatible with a good deal, including another relationship.

**Decision:** 

### 28. `lit-bereave-guilt`

- [ ] Reviewed
- *Type:* literature entry · in `addon-losing-a-partner`

> The guilt about being all right
>
> “I feel guilty for laughing again.” “I don't know if I'm allowed to be happy.” “I'm afraid moving forward means leaving them behind.”
>
> These are close to universal and they're rarely said out loud, because they sound irrational and they don't feel irrational.
>
> **What the guilt is doing:** Grief is one of the few remaining forms of contact. While it's acute, they're still present in the day.
>
> So anything that reduces it can feel like a second loss — not a relief but a further separation. That's why being all right for an afternoon can produce something that feels like betrayal.
>
> Which makes the usual reassurance land badly. “They'd want you to be happy” misses the point: the difficulty isn't whether it's permitted, it's that feeling better feels like moving away.
>
> We don't know what they'd have wanted and neither does anyone else. Nothing here will tell you what the dead would think — that's a claim nobody is entitled to make on your behalf.

**Decision:** 

### 29. `lit-bereave-nobody-understands`

- [ ] Reviewed
- *Type:* literature entry · in `addon-losing-a-partner`

> “No one understands this kind of grief”
>
> Said twice in this material, which is unusual, and we're not going to argue with it.
>
> **It's substantially accurate:** The available scripts are built for divorce. People offer them because they're what exists — you'll be better off, you'll find someone, it takes about a year.
>
> None of those are aimed at what's happened. So people who genuinely care produce things that land as though they haven't understood, because in a specific sense they haven't.
>
> There's also a category that does understand, and it's usually other people who've had the same loss. That's not a suggestion so much as an observation about where the recognition tends to come from.
>
> “I don't know who understands this kind of loss” is worth taking as a practical question rather than a rhetorical one. There are usually more people than it seems, and they aren't the obvious ones.

**Decision:** 

### 30. `lit-bereave-still-we`

- [ ] Reviewed
- *Type:* literature entry · in `addon-losing-a-partner`

> Still “we”
>
> “I still think of us as we.” “I still wear my ring.” “I don't know how to stop feeling married.” “I don't know how to introduce myself anymore.”
>
> These are the small, constant ones — the ones that arrive several times a day and that nobody warns you about.
>
> **Two different things being asked:** What's true: whether you are still married, still a we, still someone's. Those aren't questions with clean answers and they don't have to be resolved.
>

### 31. `lit-bereave-what-this-is`

- [ ] Reviewed
- *Type:* literature entry · in `addon-losing-a-partner`

> What this is for
>
> Almost everything written about relationships ending is written about relationships that ended by choice. Somebody left, or both of you did.
>
> That material fits you badly. There's no decision to examine, nobody to be angry with in the usual way, and none of the questions about what went wrong apply.
>
> **The thing that runs through most of this:** Not: how do I get over it. Very few people here are asking that.
>
> But: how do I go on living without it meaning I've left them behind. That's the question underneath the guilt, the ring, the introductions, and the wanting company.
>
> Nothing here will suggest you move on, and nothing will put a timeline on anything — in either direction. “Take all the time you need” is also a claim, and it can read as permission to stop.

**Decision:** 

### 32. `lit-c20-the-future`

- [ ] Reviewed
- *Type:* literature entry · in `finding-yourself-again`

> Grieving a future that never happened
>
> “I'm grieving the future I imagined.” “I miss the life I thought we'd have.” “I never imagined this would be my story.”
>
> This is one of the least acknowledged losses there is, because nothing happened to it. There's no event, no date, nothing anyone can point at.
>
> But you'd been living toward it — arranging things around it, declining things because of it, assuming it. That's a substantial amount of a life, organised around something that has now stopped being true.
>
> **Why it's harder to grieve than the relationship:** Nobody else can see it. There's no version of it that friends witnessed, so there's nothing to commiserate about.
>
> And it feels illegitimate — you're mourning something that never existed, which sounds like sentimentality rather than loss.
>
> It isn't sentimentality. Losing an assumed future is a recognised part of this and it accounts for a great deal of the disorientation. “I don't know what my future looks like anymore” is a fair description rather than a failure of imagination.

**Decision:** 

### 33. `lit-grievediff-different-now`

- [ ] Reviewed
- *Type:* literature entry · in `addon-grieving-differently`

> “Our relationship feels different now”
>
> It probably is. Something happened to both of you, and relationships don't pass through that unchanged.
>
> Some of what's different is grief, which is temporary in shape if not in fact. Some is the accumulated effect of months of each of you misreading the other. And some is that the future you'd both been assuming stopped being available, which is its own loss and rarely named as one.
>
> **Worth separating, because they don't move together:** The grief changes on its own timescale and doesn't respond to effort.
>
> The misreading is the part that responds to anything, and it's the part this can work on.
>
> “I feel alone in this experience” is often true even when both people are present. Being in the same house as someone grieving the same thing differently is a specific kind of alone, and it isn't evidence that either of you has withdrawn.

**Decision:** 

### 34. `lit-grievediff-supporting`

- [ ] Reviewed
- *Type:* literature entry · in `addon-grieving-differently`

> “I don't know how to support my partner”
>
> Often said by both people, about each other, at the same time. Which is worth knowing on its own.
>
> **Why the usual approach stalls:** Most people support someone the way they'd want to be supported. If you need to talk, you ask questions. If you need quiet, you give space.
>
> When two people need different things, both are offering something the other doesn't want — and both are then hurt that their offer wasn't taken.
>
> The version that helps is unglamorous: asking rather than inferring. Not “how are you”, which produces “fine”, but what they'd actually want when it's bad — which most couples have never asked each other directly.
>
> It's also worth accepting that you may not be able to support each other well through this. Two people grieving the same loss are both depleted, and sometimes the support has to come from outside. That isn't a failure of the relationship.

**Decision:** 

### 35. `lit-grievediff-the-question`

- [ ] Reviewed
- *Type:* literature entry · in `addon-grieving-differently`

> “I'm tired of everyone asking when we're having kids”
>
> A small thing that lands hard, repeatedly, from people who have no idea what they're asking.
>
> **The two costs of it:** The moment itself — being asked, and having to produce something.
>
> The anticipation. Once it's happened a few times, whole occasions get weighed in advance for whether the question will come up, which is more tiring than the question.
>
> Having a line ready mostly addresses the second. Not because the answer matters, but because knowing what you'll say removes the need to brace for it.
>
> It's also worth agreeing with each other what gets said, and to whom. Couples are frequently caught out by one of them having told someone and the other not knowing — which is a small thing that lands as a betrayal when everything is already raw.

**Decision:** 

### 36. `lit-grievediff-two-ways`

- [ ] Reviewed
- *Type:* literature entry · in `addon-grieving-differently`

> “We don't grieve the same way”
>
> This is one of the most common things couples say after a loss of this kind, and one of the most damaging — not because either way of grieving is wrong, but because each reads the other's as a verdict.
>
> **What each usually concludes about the other:** The one who talks about it hears the silence as not caring, or as having moved on.
>
> The one who doesn't hears the talking as being unable to let it rest, and often stays quiet specifically to avoid making it worse.
>
> Both are usually wrong about the other, and both are behaving reasonably given what they think is happening. Which is why it compounds — each response confirms the other's reading.
>
> Grieving differently isn't a problem to be aligned. Nothing here will suggest you grieve the same way, or that one of you should adjust. What can change is what each of you concludes from the other's way.

**Decision:**
